Each fork is placed at the estimated break-up of a common ancestor. The axis is compressed towards the past. Hover a fork for the evidence; select a language name to focus it. Three separate trees: Uralic, Indo-European and Turkic have no demonstrated common ancestor.

Polish and its Baltic neighbours

Polish, Lithuanian and Latvian share one ancestor, Proto-Balto-Slavic. Finnish and Estonian do not: what they share with Baltic and Slavic comes from thousands of years of living next door.

How to read the numbers

Separation time is today minus the estimated break-up date of the last common ancestor of two languages. It measures descent, not how alike the languages feel today.

Dates are estimates. Proto-languages left no written record. Dates come from sound-change chronology, datable loanwords, archaeology and statistical phylogenetics, and scholars often differ by centuries. Each fork carries its own range and confidence.

Spelling resemblance compares twelve basic words letter by letter. It is a rough signal only: it rewards shared spelling habits and misses true cognates that sound change has disguised (Polish serce, Lithuanian širdis).

In 3D, height is time. An ancestor floats at the height of its estimated break-up, on the same compressed scale as the tree. It hangs over the average position of its descendants. That is a layout choice, not a claimed homeland: Proto-Uralic was not spoken over central Finland.

Dots mark the rough centre of each traditional speaking area, sized by number of speakers. Hollow dots are extinct languages. Language areas overlap far more than points can show.
